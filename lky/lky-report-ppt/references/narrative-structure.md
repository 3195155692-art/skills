# Narrative structure

A literature report is an argument. If the slide list can be predicted from the paper list,
the talk has no spine and the audience will not remember it.

## Find the through-line first

Read every source note, then answer one question: **what changed, step by step, across these
papers?** Write the answer as a sentence with a direction. Everything else is decoration.

Two through-lines that worked:

- *"Each step forward came not from better decoding, but from a stricter control condition."*
  The papers were ordered by the evidence standard they had to meet, not by date or journal.
- *"The system's relationship to the patient changed: a test, then a channel, then a loop."*
  The papers were ordered by what the BCI does to the person, not by method.

Pick one, state it on the outline page, and return to it at the end.

## Use acts, not a paper list

For a report covering more than about four papers, group them into two to four acts, each
opening with a full-bleed divider page that states what that act has to establish. The
divider is not decoration: it tells the audience where the argument is and lets a listener
who lost the thread re-enter.

Give each act a **different kind of move** and say so on the divider. "Act 1 changes the
question; act 2 changes the control condition; act 3 changes the relationship." If two acts
make the same move, they are one act.

## Give every paper a closing line

End each paper's section with a visible one-line takeaway — the sentence you would want the
audience to repeat to someone else. Then collect all of them on a single summary slide near
the end, so the deck doubles as an index for the discussion. Use the note's own
`汇报建议`/`汇报结论` section as the source; those lines are usually already written.

Also carry **title, one-sentence conclusion, in-the-arc position** for each paper on the
summary slide. Three columns are enough.

## Layer the explanation

Order slides so each one answers the question the previous one raised, and let a page exist
only if it moves that chain forward. When a concept is load-bearing — a definition, a
distinction, a failure mode — give it a full page rather than a bullet on someone else's
page. When a page merely restates the previous one with a different example, cut it.

Keep a short route back to the spine: after a dense stretch, one line saying which part of
the argument just advanced.

## Separate the authors from yourself

The audience must always be able to tell what a paper claims from what you concluded.

- Quote or paraphrase an author's claim plainly.
- Label your own synthesis `本汇报推论` on the slide, in the same visual style each time.
- On the final synthesis slide, add a line naming which of your inferences you would
  actually pursue, and say the whole page is your reading rather than a paper's conclusion.

## Keep the uncomfortable evidence

The strongest thing a report can do is keep the facts that weaken its own headline. Carry
these into the deck deliberately:

- the control condition that did badly (it bounds the headline number);
- the two numbers in the paper that disagree with each other;
- the subgroup that did not replicate, and the authors' own competing explanations;
- the sources that are abstract-only, priced in visibly on the slide and in the summary.

Put the qualifying number next to the headline, not in an appendix. If a paper reports one
loud number and one quiet number that limits it, show both in the same glance.

## Close on the new direction

End the arc where the user asked the story to land — for a BCI report, that is usually the
closed loop: detect, intervene, re-detect. Give that page the strongest treatment in the
deck (a dark page works well) and state both what the direction solves and what it costs:
closing the loop creates a causal handle, and destroys the independence of the reference
point. Naming the cost is what makes the ending land rather than sound like a sales pitch.

Then leave three specific, arguable questions for the group rather than a generic thanks.
